## 2.2  Planning and design
Upon identification of the stressors affecting a peatland and their extent, restoration planning can begin. This is a critical phase, as it defines the project vision, while identifying potential problems and conflicts that might be encountered along the way.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-4b-origin" href="#fn-4">[4]</a>,<a class="fnref" id="ref-19-origin" href="#fn-19">[19]</a>,<a class="fnref" id="ref-1g-origin" href="#fn-1">[1]</a></sup> Developing a restoration plan is a complex process, requiring engagement with stakeholders, definition of clear goals and objectives, prioritization of restoration options, and effective baseline data. Incorporating these components into the planning phase sets the stage for effective peatland restoration.

### Stakeholder engagement
Peatland restoration requires balancing, in addition to ecological objectives, socioeconomic, cultural, and legal considerations. Therefore, effective planning of restoration projects is dependent on engagement with stakeholders who have an investment in the project outcome.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-8j-origin" href="#fn-8">[8]</a></sup> For a list of common stakeholder groups that should be engaged during restoration projects, along with reasons for engagement, see Table X.

  <p>During the planning process, conflicts are likely to arise regarding the desired outcomes of restoration and how these outcomes will be achieved. Engagement is critical for navigating these conflicts, as evidenced by a case study presented by Pakalne et al. (2021).<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-10b-origin" href="#fn-10">[10]</a></sup> In the Estonian LIFE Peat Restore project, restoration of degraded peatlands was considered a national priority. At one project site, two ditches were planned to be blocked for rewetting. However, these ditches were identified as critical spawning streams for salmon and trout, and therefore, protected under law. The restorationists were stuck between two conflicting directives: protecting spawning streams and restoring degraded peatlands. Eventually, after nearly two years of engagement and discussion with stakeholders, a compromise was reached in which restoration continued while preserving spawning stream function and integrity. This case highlights that although engagement can be a lengthy and complex process, it is essential for addressing the needs and concerns of stakeholders to achieve effective restoration.</p>

Further, effective restoration requires continuous engagement throughout the entire process, not just during the planning phase. Communication channels with stakeholders established early on must be well-maintained and prepared for an adaptive approach. This ensures that unforeseen conditions can be quickly addressed, minimizing project delays.

### Goals and objectives
Before restoration treatments, practitioners must determine what the project is trying to achieve. Two levels of target are defined in peatland restoration:

* **Goals** are general statements indicating the desired medium to long-term ecological or socioeconomic restoration outcomes of the project (e.g., "restore a functioning, peat-forming mire community"; "Reduce GHG emissions on site by X%").<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-4c-origin" href="#fn-4">[4]</a></sup>
* **Objectives** are well-defined, precise statements focused on measurable parameters which outline how the goal(s) will be achieved (e.g., "raise the mean annual water table to within 20 cm of the surface across 80% of the restoration site within five years of intervention").<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-17-origin" href="#fn-17">[17]</a></sup>

Setting goals and objectives requires developing a **reference model**: a conceptual framework that provides a quantitative representation of the conditions a site could exhibit if it were not degraded. Reference models compile information from multiple sources, including physical reference sites, historical conditions, current conditions, and stakeholder engagement.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-4d-origin" href="#fn-4">[4]</a></sup> As reference models do not aim to describe the past conditions of a site, but rather the potential conditions that would exist if restoration were achieved, factors that could impact the site's future functioning (e.g., climate change) can be incorporated. With a well-developed reference model, desired post-restoration conditions can be established and realistic goals and objectives can be formulated to ensure effective restoration.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-1h-origin" href="#fn-1">[1]</a>,<a class="fnref" id="ref-7c-origin" href="#fn-7">[7]</a></sup>

Setting realistic and effective goals and objectives requires consultation with stakeholders to navigate and avoid conflicts.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-8k-origin" href="#fn-8">[8]</a></sup> To facilitate consultation, goals and objectives are often kept simple to ensure stakeholders understand and can effectively contribute to the goal-setting process.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-17b-origin" href="#fn-17">[17]</a></sup> Common goal themes in European peatland restoration include climate change mitigation, biodiversity conservation, productivity (i.e., paludiculture), and water quality improvement, and flood control.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-8l-origin" href="#fn-8">[8]</a></sup> Goals can focus on specific, measurable ecological conditions or more broadly on ecosystem services (Figure x from Section 1.2).

### Prioritisation
When the restoration potential of a site exceeds the available budget or feasible intervention area, goals must be ranked by priority.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-8m-origin" href="#fn-8">[8]</a></sup> For a large, highly degraded peatland, it may not be feasible to restore the entire area, requiring prioritisation of restoration treatments and goals. Restoring highly degraded sites is more costly than restoring moderately degraded sites; thus, a project with a large budget is likely to have greater restoration effectiveness targeting more degraded areas. Conversely, smaller budget projects can still achieve restoration by targeting moderately degraded areas where the required effort is lower and restoration is more cost-effective.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-11b-origin" href="#fn-11">[11]</a></sup> The complexity of prioritising restoration goals necessitates a dynamic approach. A case-by-case evaluation should be done for each project to determine the best course of restorative action using knowledge of the site, well-developed reference models, and engagement with stakeholders.

### Restoration plan
After clear goals and objectives have been established and prioritised, they are formalised in a restoration plan. A well-developed plan is critical for achieving project outcomes<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-19b-origin" href="#fn-19">[19]</a>,<a class="fnref" id="ref-1i-origin" href="#fn-1">[1]</a></sup> while supporting cost-effective restoration by helping to avoid inefficient field work. A restoration plan should include the following:

* **Site description** — location, boundary, topography, hydrology, soil, fauna, flora, current land use, stakeholders, stressors, and level of degradation;
* **Goals and objectives** — desired outcomes with clearly defined, measurable benchmarks;
* **Restoration measures** — specific treatments, maps, schedules, materials, contractors, and budgets;
* **Monitoring plan** — protocols, indicators, measurement thresholds, and responsibilities;
* **Maintenance plan** — ongoing work required to prevent site regression post-treatment.

The restoration plan should be considered a living document that will change and evolve throughout the project.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-19c-origin" href="#fn-19">[19]</a></sup> Incorporation of adaptive management, the systematic process of using feedback from the environment to continuously adjust and improve management strategies,<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-4e-origin" href="#fn-4">[4]</a></sup> ensures that the plan is constantly evolving.

### Baseline monitoring
The final step before restoration measures can begin is to conduct baseline monitoring. This provides a point of comparison, both for assessing pre-restoration conditions against the reference model and for comparing pre-restoration and post-restoration conditions. Having these comparisons is necessary for measuring the effectiveness of restoration and communicating project development to stakeholders. As pre-restoration conditions will be compared to post-restoration conditions, baseline monitoring should focus on measurable indicators which will determine if desired restoration outcomes have been achieved.<sup><a class="fnref" id="ref-4f-origin" href="#fn-4">[4]</a>,<a class="fnref" id="ref-1j-origin" href="#fn-1">[1]</a></sup> For guidance on monitoring design and parameter selection, see Section 2.5.
